\chapter{Related Work}
\label{chap:related-work}

This chapter positions the paper at the centre of this project---\emph{TFPnP: Tuning-free
Plug-and-Play Proximal Algorithms with Applications to Inverse Imaging Problems}
\citep{Wei2022TFPnP}---within the broader landscape of CT reconstruction methods. We
trace the intellectual lineage from classical iterative methods through variational
regularisation and plug-and-play frameworks to the deep learning methods against which
TFPnP is benchmarked. We then provide a detailed technical and critical analysis of the
paper itself.

% -----------------------------------------------------------
\section{Classical Iterative Methods}
\label{sec:classical-iterative}
% -----------------------------------------------------------

As described in \cref{sec:classical-reconstruction}, iterative methods such as SIRT,
ART (Algebraic Reconstruction Technique) \citep{Gordon1970}, and CGLS (Conjugate
Gradient Least Squares) minimise the data-fidelity term
$\frac{1}{2}\|A\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_2^2$ without an explicit regulariser. Their convergence
properties are well understood, but they are limited by the ill-posedness of the inverse
problem: without regularisation, they overfit to noise given sufficiently many iterations.
Early stopping acts as an implicit regulariser, introducing a trade-off between data
consistency and noise suppression that is difficult to tune automatically.

% -----------------------------------------------------------
\section{Variational Regularisation}
\label{sec:variational}
% -----------------------------------------------------------

Variational methods frame reconstruction as a regularised optimisation problem:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\bm{x}} = \arg\min_{\bm{x}}\; D(\bm{y}, A\bm{x}) + \lambda R(\bm{x}),
    \label{eq:variational}
\end{equation}
where $D$ is a data-fidelity term (e.g., $\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_2^2$ for Gaussian noise,
or a Kullback--Leibler divergence for Poisson noise) and $R$ is a regulariser that
encodes prior knowledge about $\bm{x}$. The parameter $\lambda > 0$ controls the
trade-off between data fit and regularity. A rich hierarchy of regularisers has been
studied \citep{Benning2018}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tikhonov regularisation} \citep{TikhonovArsenin1977}:
          $R(\bm{x}) = \frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}\|_2^2$. Penalises energy; yields smooth
          solutions; closed-form inversion via $(A^TA + \lambda I)^{-1}A^T\bm{y}$.
    \item \textbf{Total variation} \citep{RudinOsherFatemi1992}:
          $R(\bm{x}) = \|\nabla\bm{x}\|_1$. Promotes piecewise-constant images;
          well-suited to CT; solved by PDHG or ADMM.
    \item \textbf{Sparsity-promoting regularisers}: Wavelet $\ell^1$ penalties
          \citep{Mallat1989}, dictionary learning \citep{Mairal2009}, and low-rank
          regularisation \citep{Fazel2001}.
\end{itemize}

The key limitation of all explicit regularisers is that their functional forms embody
strong modelling assumptions---piecewise constancy for TV, wavelet sparsity for wavelet
penalties---that may not hold for complex anatomical structures. Furthermore, $\lambda$
must be chosen per-problem, a process that is non-trivial in clinical practice.

\section{Proximal Algorithms}
\label{sec:proximal-algorithms}
% -----------------------------------------------------------

When $R$ is non-smooth (as for TV), standard gradient descent cannot be applied.
\emph{Proximal algorithms} decompose the composite minimisation into subproblems
involving the \emph{proximal operator} \citep{Moreau1962,Parikh2014}:
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Prox}_{\gamma R}(\bm{v})
        = \arg\min_{\bm{x}}\;\gamma R(\bm{x}) + \frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{v}\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:prox}
\end{equation}
For many regularisers, \eqref{eq:prox} has a closed-form solution (e.g.,
soft-thresholding for $R = \|\cdot\|_1$).

\paragraph{Proximal gradient descent (Forward--Backward splitting).}
When $D$ is smooth with $L$-Lipschitz gradient and $R$ is non-smooth, the iterates are:
\begin{equation}
    \bm{x}^{k+1} = \operatorname{Prox}_{\gamma R}\!\left(\bm{x}^k - \gamma \nabla D(\bm{x}^k)\right).
\end{equation}
FISTA \citep{BeckTeboulle2009} adds Nesterov momentum to achieve $\mathcal{O}(1/k^2)$
convergence.

\paragraph{ADMM.}
When both $D$ and $R$ are non-smooth, the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers
(ADMM) \citep{GabayMercier1976,Boyd2011} introduces an auxiliary variable $\bm{z}$ to
decouple $D$ and $R$:
\begin{align}
    \bm{x}^{k+1} &= \operatorname{Prox}_{\sigma^2 R}(\bm{z}^k - \bm{u}^k),
        \label{eq:admm-x}\\
    \bm{z}^{k+1} &= \operatorname{Prox}_{\frac{1}{\mu}D}(\bm{x}^{k+1} + \bm{u}^k),
        \label{eq:admm-z}\\
    \bm{u}^{k+1} &= \bm{u}^k + \bm{x}^{k+1} - \bm{z}^{k+1},
        \label{eq:admm-u}
\end{align}
where $\mu > 0$ is a penalty parameter and $\sigma^2 = \lambda/\mu$. ADMM alternates
between an image-domain regularisation update~\eqref{eq:admm-x}, a
data-consistency update~\eqref{eq:admm-z}, and a dual variable
update~\eqref{eq:admm-u}.

\section{Plug-and-Play (PnP) Methods}
\label{sec:pnp}
% -----------------------------------------------------------

The \emph{Plug-and-Play} (PnP) framework \citep{Venkatakrishnan2013} is the immediate
precursor to TFPnP and deserves careful treatment.

\subsection{The PnP Insight}

The $\bm{x}$-update in ADMM~\eqref{eq:admm-x} is precisely the proximal operator of
$R$ applied to $\bm{z}^k - \bm{u}^k$. By~\eqref{eq:prox}, this is equivalent to a
denoising problem: given a noisy observation $\bm{v} = \bm{z}^k - \bm{u}^k$, find the
image that minimises $\sigma^2 R(\bm{x}) + \frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{v}\|_2^2$. The PnP
insight is to replace this proximal step with \emph{any} off-the-shelf denoiser
$\mathcal{H}_\sigma$, regardless of whether it corresponds to an explicit $R$:
\begin{equation}
    \bm{x}^{k+1} = \mathcal{H}_{\sigma_k}(\bm{z}^k - \bm{u}^k).
    \label{eq:pnp-admm-x}
\end{equation}
The resulting \emph{PnP-ADMM} scheme is:
\begin{align}
    \bm{x}^{k+1} &= \mathcal{H}_{\sigma_k}(\bm{z}^k - \bm{u}^k), \label{eq:pnpadmm1}\\
    \bm{z}^{k+1} &= \operatorname{Prox}_{\frac{1}{\mu_k}D}(\bm{x}^{k+1} + \bm{u}^k),
        \label{eq:pnpadmm2}\\
    \bm{u}^{k+1} &= \bm{u}^k + \bm{x}^{k+1} - \bm{z}^{k+1}. \label{eq:pnpadmm3}
\end{align}
This framework decouples the forward model (handled in the $\bm{z}$-step) from the
image prior (handled via the denoiser in the $\bm{x}$-step), allowing state-of-the-art
denoisers---BM3D \citep{Dabov2007}, NLM, or deep CNN denoisers such as DnCNN
\citep{Zhang2017DnCNN}---to be swapped in without retraining for each new inverse
problem.

\subsection{PnP Variants and Extensions}

The body of PnP literature extends the framework in several directions
\citep{Wei2022TFPnP}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Proximal algorithms.} PnP has been combined with ADMM
          \citep{Venkatakrishnan2013}, half-quadratic splitting (HQS)
          \citep{Zhang2017IRCNN}, proximal gradient (PnP-PGM) \citep{Sun2019online},
          accelerated proximal gradient (PnP-APGM) \citep{Kamilov2017}, and their
          stochastic variants.
    \item \textbf{Regularisation by Denoising (RED).} \citet{Romano2017} proposed an
          explicit regulariser $R(\bm{x}) = \frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^T\!(\bm{x} -
          \mathcal{H}_\sigma(\bm{x}))$ whose gradient $\nabla R(\bm{x}) = \bm{x} -
          \mathcal{H}_\sigma(\bm{x})$ is the denoising residual, enabling gradient-based
          optimisation.
    \item \textbf{Denoising-based AMP (D-AMP).} \citet{Metzler2016} adapts the
          Approximate Message Passing framework by replacing its thresholding step with
          a deep denoiser, exploiting the Onsager correction to track effective noise
          statistics.
    \item \textbf{Theoretical convergence.} Convergence of PnP algorithms is challenging
          to prove because denoisers do not generally correspond to the proximal operator
          of any convex function. Progress has been made under assumptions of bounded or
          firmly non-expansive denoisers \citep{Ryu2019}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Parameter Tuning Problem}

Despite their empirical success, PnP algorithms suffer a fundamental practical
limitation: their performance is highly sensitive to the choice of internal
parameters---principally the denoising strength $\sigma_k$ at each iteration, the
penalty parameter $\mu_k$, and the termination time $\tau$. As demonstrated in
Figure~1 of \citet{Wei2022TFPnP}, the optimal PSNR for each image in a dataset occurs
at a \emph{different} parameter setting, and sometimes even requires early stopping to
avoid quality degradation. No single fixed parameter schedule achieves uniformly good
performance across images with varying noise levels and content. This
\emph{manual tuning problem} is the precise gap that TFPnP is designed to fill.

\section{Algorithm Unrolling and Learned Primal Dual}
\label{sec:lpd}
% -----------------------------------------------------------

\emph{Algorithm unrolling} \citep{GregorLeCun2010} is a complementary paradigm that
unrolls a fixed number of optimisation iterations into a deep neural network, replacing
hand-crafted components with learned ones and training the entire unrolled system
end-to-end. Learned Primal Dual (LPD) \citep{AdlerOktem2018} is the leading
algorithm-unrolling method for CT reconstruction and the primary benchmark against
which TFPnP is compared.

LPD replaces the proximal operators of a primal-dual algorithm with small CNNs:
\begin{align}
    h^{k+1} &= \Gamma^k_\phi\!\left(h^k,\; A\bm{x}^k,\; \bm{y}\right), \\
    g^{k+1} &= \Lambda^k_\theta\!\left(g^k,\; A^T h^{k+1}\right),\\
    \bm{x}^{k+1} &= \bm{x}^k + g^{k+1},
\end{align}
where $\Gamma^k_\phi$ and $\Lambda^k_\theta$ are small CNNs (with unshared weights
across iterations) operating in measurement space and image space respectively, and
$h^k, g^k$ are running memory variables. The network is trained end-to-end by
minimising $\|\bm{x}^K - \bm{x}^*\|_2^2$ over paired training data $(\bm{y}, \bm{x}^*)$.

LPD is state-of-the-art in supervised CT reconstruction performance, achieving
22.72/22.95/22.50\,dB PSNR at the three noise levels in the TFPnP benchmark
\citep{Wei2022TFPnP}. However, it requires a separate trained model for each acquisition
geometry and noise level; it cannot generalise across measurement conditions without
retraining, unlike PnP methods.

\section{Deep Learning for CT Reconstruction}
\label{sec:dl-ct}
% -----------------------------------------------------------

The broader field of deep learning for CT reconstruction can be taxonomised along two axes:
(i) the degree to which the forward model $A$ is incorporated into the architecture, and
(ii) whether the method is supervised or unsupervised. The benchmarks used in the TFPnP
CT experiments \citep{Wei2022TFPnP} span this taxonomy:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FBP + post-processing (FBPconv)} \citep{Jin2017}: applies FBP to
          produce an initial reconstruction, then trains a CNN to remove artefacts in
          image space. Fast but does not enforce data consistency. Achieves 22.16\,dB.
    \item \textbf{RED-CNN} \citep{Chen2017RED}: a CNN trained in image space using
          residual learning to denoise low-dose CT reconstructions. Achieves 22.34\,dB.
          Does not use the forward model during inference.
    \item \textbf{RPGD} \citep{Gupta2018}: combines projected gradient descent
          steps (using $A$ and $A^T$) with a CNN denoiser trained as a proximal
          operator. Enforces data consistency; achieves 23.19\,dB.
    \item \textbf{LPD} \citep{AdlerOktem2018}: as described in \cref{sec:lpd};
          achieves 22.72\,dB.
\end{itemize}

\section{TFPnP: Tuning-free Plug-and-Play Proximal Algorithm}
\label{sec:tfpnp}
% -----------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Paper Overview and Positioning}

The paper ``TFPnP: Tuning-free Plug-and-Play Proximal Algorithms with Applications to
Inverse Imaging Problems'' \citep{Wei2022TFPnP} was published in the \emph{Journal of
Machine Learning Research} (Volume~23, 2022) by Kaixuan Wei, Angelica Aviles-Rivero,
Jingwei Liang, Ying Fu, Hua Huang, and Carola-Bibiane Sch\"{o}nlieb. It extends a
conference paper at ICML 2020 \citep{Wei2020a}.

The paper sits at the intersection of three traditions: PnP methods (exploiting the
modular separation of forward model and denoiser prior), reinforcement learning (used
as the optimisation engine for parameter search), and inverse imaging (validated on
CS-MRI, sparse-view CT, single-photon imaging, and phase retrieval). Its core thesis is
that the internal parameter selection in PnP algorithms---previously treated as manual
hyperparameters or governed by handcrafted heuristics---can be framed as a
\emph{sequential decision-making problem} and solved by a learned policy network.

\subsection{Main Contributions}

The paper makes three principal contributions \citep{Wei2022TFPnP}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The TFPnP framework.} A class of PnP algorithms in which the parameter
          selection (denoising strength $\sigma_k$, penalty $\mu_k$, and termination time
          $\tau$) is governed by a learned policy $\pi$, rather than fixed or handcrafted
          schedules. The framework is generic: it is instantiated for PnP-ADMM, PnP-HQS,
          PnP-PGM, PnP-APGM, RED-ADMM, and D-AMP.
    \item \textbf{A mixed model-free and model-based RL algorithm.} The policy network
          has two sub-policies: a stochastic sub-policy $\pi_1$ for the discrete
          termination decision (stop or continue), trained with model-free policy
          gradients; and a deterministic sub-policy $\pi_2$ for the continuous parameters
          ($\sigma_k$, $\mu_k$), trained via model-based backpropagation through the
          differentiable PnP-ADMM iterations.
    \item \textbf{Extensive empirical validation.} State-of-the-art results on CS-MRI,
          sparse-view CT, single-photon imaging, and phase retrieval, with ablations of
          the denoiser prior choice, policy design, and hyperparameter sensitivity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Technical Details: The RL Formulation}

The TFPnP algorithm is built on a Markov Decision Process (MDP) formulation
$(S, A, p, r)$ \citep{Wei2022TFPnP}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{State space $\mathcal{S}$.} The current optimisation state
          $s^k = (\bm{x}^k, \bm{z}^k, \bm{u}^k)$, augmented with scalars encoding the
          noise level and the iteration count.
    \item \textbf{Action space $\mathcal{A}$.} Actions are pairs $(a_1, a_2)$ where
          $a_1 \in \{0, 1\}$ is the termination decision and
          $a_2 = (\sigma_k, \mu_k)$ are the continuous parameters.
    \item \textbf{Transition function $p$.} Defined by $m$ consecutive PnP-ADMM
          iterations; differentiable with respect to the U-Net denoiser
          \citep{Ronneberger2015}.
    \item \textbf{Reward function $r$.} A scalar measuring image quality improvement
          relative to the previous state, encouraging policies that reach high-quality
          reconstructions efficiently and terminate at the optimal iteration.
\end{itemize}

The objective is:
\begin{equation}
    J(\pi)
        = \mathbb{E}_{s^0 \sim \mathcal{S}_0,\, \mathcal{T} \sim \pi}\!\left[R_0\right],
    \label{eq:rl-objective}
\end{equation}
where $R_0 = \sum_{t'=0}^{N-1} \rho^{t'} r(s^{t+t'}, a^{t+t'})$ is the discounted
return and $\rho \in [0,1]$ is a discount factor. The policy and value networks are
trained jointly: the value network $V^\pi_\phi$ minimises the Bellman residual, while
$\pi_1$ is trained via the REINFORCE policy gradient theorem and $\pi_2$ is trained via
deterministic policy gradient through the differentiable environment.

\subsection{Key Innovations}

\paragraph{Joint optimisation of discrete and continuous parameters.}
Prior automated parameter selection methods (L-curve \citep{Hansen1992}, generalised
cross-validation, Stein's unbiased risk estimator) targeted a single fixed parameter
for all iterations. TFPnP produces a \emph{schedule} of parameters adapting to the
current optimisation state.

\paragraph{Model-based gradient estimation for continuous parameters.}
Using the differentiable U-Net denoiser, TFPnP backpropagates through the PnP
iterations to compute the exact gradient of the return with respect to $(\sigma_k,
\mu_k)$. This model-based gradient has far lower variance than Monte Carlo estimates,
substantially accelerating training of the continuous sub-policy.

\paragraph{Generalisation across imaging conditions.}
Because the policy encodes the noise level and iteration count as auxiliary inputs, a
\emph{single} trained policy generalises across different noise levels and undersampling
factors---a property that supervised end-to-end methods such as LPD lack by design.

\paragraph{Early stopping as a first-class decision.}
TFPnP treats the termination time as a learnable decision, allowing the policy to stop
as soon as further iterations would degrade quality due to noise overfitting. This
is a critical robustness property.

\subsection{Comparison to Prior Work}

\paragraph{vs.\ classical PnP.}
Classical PnP-ADMM with a fixed parameter schedule (e.g., the IRCNN handcrafted
schedule of \citet{Zhang2017IRCNN}) achieves sub-optimal PSNR because no single
schedule serves all images. TFPnP demonstrates that the learned policy reaches results
close to the \emph{oracle policy}---one that has access to the ground truth to set
parameters---without any ground truth at test time.

\paragraph{vs.\ supervised deep learning (LPD, RED-CNN, FBPconv).}
Supervised methods require \emph{paired} training data and produce a fixed function from
sinogram to image. TFPnP's denoiser can be trained on image-space patches (unpaired
with sinograms) and the RL policy is trained separately; this modular training is both
more data-efficient and more interpretable. TFPnP also inherits data consistency from
the $\bm{z}$-step~\eqref{eq:pnpadmm2}, whereas image-domain methods (RED-CNN,
FBPconv) do not enforce $\|A\bm{x} - \bm{y}\|_2 \leq \varepsilon$.

\paragraph{vs.\ algorithm unrolling (LPD).}
Both methods incorporate the forward operator $A$ during inference. The key difference
is that LPD's number of unrolled iterations is fixed at training time; TFPnP's
termination time is adaptive and learned. Additionally, TFPnP's policy can adapt to
unseen noise levels, whereas LPD requires retraining for each new acquisition condition.

\subsection{Assumptions and Limitations}

A critical reading of TFPnP reveals several important assumptions and gaps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gaussian denoiser mismatch.} The PnP denoiser $\mathcal{H}_\sigma$ is
          trained for Gaussian noise, but the \emph{iteration noise}
          $\varepsilon^k = \bar{\bm{x}}^k - \bm{x}^*$ is not Gaussian in early
          iterations. The paper shows empirically that iteration noise becomes
          increasingly Gaussian as iterations proceed, and that PnP-ADMM achieves the
          best Gaussianisation ($R^2 = 0.972$)---partially justifying the choice, but
          not eliminating the mismatch.
    \item \textbf{CT geometry limitation.} The TFPnP paper uses TorchRadon
          \citep{Ronchetti2020} with a simplified 30-view parallel-beam geometry.
          Generalisation to clinical cone-beam geometries is a gap that the present
          project, using LION/tomosipo \citep{Biguri2016TIGRE,Hendriksen2021tomosipo},
          is positioned to address.
    \item \textbf{Training cost.} Training the RL policy is substantially more expensive
          than training a supervised network, as it requires differentiating through many
          PnP iterations. Training times are not reported, making reproducibility
          assessment difficult.
    \item \textbf{No convergence guarantee for the full system.} While convergence of
          PnP-ADMM under certain denoiser conditions is established
          \citep{Ryu2019,Sun2021}, the addition of a learned adaptive policy breaks the
          assumptions of existing proofs. TFPnP is validated empirically, not
          theoretically.
    \item \textbf{2D only.} All CT experiments are 2D (slice-by-slice), without
          addressing volumetric 3D reconstruction.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What the Paper Does Not Claim}

TFPnP is presented as a \emph{generic algorithmic framework}, not a CT-specific method.
The paper does not claim optimality for any specific CT geometry beyond the 30-view
parallel-beam benchmark, demonstrate clinical applicability on patient data, or address
3D reconstruction. These are the precise gaps that the present implementation project
is directly positioned to investigate.
